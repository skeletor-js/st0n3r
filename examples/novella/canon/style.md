## Voice

<!-- Register, diction, sentence-level personality. Formal or plain? Wry or

Restrained, close, unsentimental third person that trusts concrete labor and object detail to carry feeling the narration won't state outright. Diction is plain and specific to the craft of growing (soil, light cycles, curing, trimming, county code) rather than lyrical or druggy. The prose favors declarative fact over metaphor; when it does turn figurative, the figure comes from farm work, not nature-worship. Example sentences: 'She had grown cannabis for forty years and understood, the way she understood weather, that none of it had ever really been about the money.' 'The permit renewal sat on the kitchen table for six days before she opened it, and when she did, she read it standing up, twice, before she let herself sit down.' 'There was a way of trimming a bud that had taken her eleven years to learn properly, and the buyer's spreadsheet did not have a column for it.'

## Sentence Rhythm

<!-- Guidance at the sentence and paragraph level: preferred sentence-length
     variance ("burstiness" — mix short punches with longer runs), typical
     paragraph length, how much white space, when to break rhythm on
     purpose for emphasis. The slop detector flags uniform sentence length;
     tell the model what "varied" means for *this* book. -->

Alternate long, accretive, procedural sentences that pile up years or steps of labor (mirroring Stoner's plain-style compression of time) with short, flat declaratives placed at emotional turns to land hard without comment. Let scenes open in concrete present action — hands doing something specific — before any reflection is permitted, and cap interiority at two or three sentences before returning to task or object. Several chapters should end by pulling back into summary or time-lapse (a season, a year) rather than a scene button, the way Stoner moves through decades in a paragraph. Avoid cathartic emotional climaxes; let the sentence simply stop.
