## Act I — Setup

<!-- Inciting incident, what's at stake, the world before it breaks. -->

Ruth Vann, sixty-one, has run an unpermitted cannabis operation on the same Humboldt parcel for forty years. Legalization arrives not as freedom but as paperwork: a county permit packet listing setbacks, water certification, and track-and-trace requirements she has no framework for, and a buyout letter from a corporate operator, Canopy, naming a real dollar figure for land and knowledge she has never priced. She hires a young hand, Wyatt, half hoping he might be someone to teach. A site inspection turns her forty-year-old infrastructure — built for concealment, not code — into a checklist of violations. The act ends with her private operation now officially itemized by strangers, and no way to un-know that.

## Act II — Confrontation

<!-- Rising complications, midpoint reversal, the low point. -->

Every attempt to comply reveals a further requirement beneath it: an unpermitted water diversion with no affordable legal path, a corrective-action list that prices her out fix by fix, a neighbor's parcel already absorbed by Canopy without friction. Wyatt proves competent but transient, checking corporate wage postings; her daughter Iris visits only to confirm she never wanted the farm; her old friend Denny, already sold and retired, names the shape of what's happening — that legality is only prohibition's quieter method of taking the same thing. The midpoint reversal lands not as a denial but as arithmetic: compliance, line by line, is simply unaffordable, foreclosing the legal path without anyone saying no. A late-season storm forces Ruth to save the crop alone through the night, proving to herself, if no one else, what her hands still know that no spreadsheet accounts for — the low point resolved internally even as every external system still says she has already lost.

## Act III — Resolution

<!-- Climax, the central question answered, the promise to the reader
     paid off. -->

Canopy returns with a slightly higher offer and a consulting clause that would require Ruth to translate her uncodified craft — the trim technique she wouldn't even give Wyatt — into their standardized process. She refuses the clause, drawing her first clear line in the book, though the land offer stays open. She brings in her final harvest the old way, deciding without announcing it, mid-task, as she has always decided things. At the county hearing, the panel denies the full permit but grants a conditional, reduced-scale license, answering the central question plainly: forty years of exacting labor cannot survive legalization at its former scale, only in diminished, sanctioned form, or not at all. The novel closes on Ruth curing the last of the crop by hand, in the same shed, holding the one thing no permit could grant or revoke — not triumph, not defeat, a life fully seen on its own terms.
