title: The Queue

Ruth left Wyatt with a list Friday morning — top the third row of mothers, check the drip lines on the full-sun plot for the leak she'd heard but not found, do not touch the generator shed — and drove the truck down the spur and out onto Kettle Rock Road before the fog had lifted off the creek. She had not told him where she was going. She had told herself it was because there was nothing yet to tell, which was true, and also because saying it out loud to another person made it a thing that was happening rather than a thing she was still deciding whether to do, and she wasn't ready to give up that distinction.

The drive to Eureka took an hour and ten minutes on a good day, longer behind a log truck, and she had budgeted two hours each way without deciding what she'd do with the extra time if the trip went faster than that. She had brought the packet, all eleven pages plus the cover letter, in a manila envelope on the seat beside her, and a second sheet of her own where she'd written out, the night before, in the block printing she used for anything she meant to keep, the four questions she wanted answered in plain numbers. What she would owe to bring the shed into setback. What she would owe on the water. Whether the reduced-license path could be walked back later if she cured the rest. Whether there was any route at all that let her keep the full-sun plot.

She had priced fence line and fuel and hired labor for forty years without needing anyone else's number to check her own arithmetic against, and it had not occurred to her until she was most of the way to Fortuna that she was driving an hour and ten minutes to ask a stranger behind a counter to confirm math she had mostly already done herself, standing at her own kitchen table, twice.

The Planning and Building Department sat on H Street in a two-story building the color of a manila folder, flags out front, a wheelchair ramp with a rust stain running down one rail where the gutter above it had failed and never been fixed. Inside, the permit counter took up half the ground floor, a row of numbered windows behind a rope line, and a paper dispenser with a red button that spat out ticket stubs, and she pulled one — forty-one — and looked at the digital sign above the windows, which read nineteen, and found a folding chair.

She waited fifty minutes. The chairs were bolted to a rail along the wall so that no one could carry one off, and the rail had a give to it when she leaned back, the same forgiving half-inch as the chair at her own kitchen table, and she noticed that and then made herself stop noticing it, because it was the kind of thing that could occupy a mind that didn't want to think about what it had driven two hours to ask. Around her sat men in canvas jackets with folders held shut by rubber bands, a woman with a toddler asleep against her shoulder, a man in a fleece vest with a laptop bag who did not look at his phone once in the whole time Ruth watched him, which she found, without deciding to find it, more unsettling than if he had.

When her number came up she carried the envelope to window six and set it on the counter, unopened, the way she'd have set a scale reading in front of a buyer she trusted to read it straight.

The woman on the other side of the glass had a badge that said PRATT and a headset she didn't take off, and she pulled the packet toward her with two fingers before Ruth had finished explaining what it was, and said, "This is the standard cultivation compliance packet, ma'am, everybody in the watershed's getting this one," in the tone of someone correcting a fact that hadn't been in dispute.

"I've read it," Ruth said. "I've got specific numbers I need. My generator shed's short on the parcel-line setback and short on the watercourse setback both. I need to know what it costs me to cure that, not what the form says, what it actually runs, and I need the same for the water — the tank, installed, not the sticker price on the tank alone."

"I can't give you contractor pricing," Pratt said. "That's not something the county quotes. You'd want an engineer or a licensed contractor for a site-specific bid."

"I understand that. I'm asking what the county charges. The reinspection fee, the plan review, whatever comes after the twelve hundred if the first plan gets kicked back."

Pratt turned to a screen Ruth couldn't see and typed for a while, longer than the question seemed to need, and printed a single sheet from a machine behind her that hummed and then clicked twice, and slid it under the glass. A fee schedule. Category Two reinspection, twelve hundred dollars. Engineered remediation plan review, four hundred, non-refundable, due whether or not the plan was approved. A second reinspection, if the first plan failed, another twelve hundred. No line anywhere on the sheet for what the wall itself would cost to build, because the county did not build walls, the county only came out afterward and measured whether the wall that got built was far enough from the line.

"And the reduced license," Ruth said. "If I take the five thousand square feet this year because I can't cure everything in time, is that a door that closes, or can I come back once I've cured the rest and get back to what I've got now?"

Pratt looked at something on her screen a while before answering, which Ruth took, correctly or not, as evidence that the answer wasn't simple even for the person paid to know it. "You'd reapply for an expansion once you're fully compliant. It's not automatic. There's a new environmental review triggered any time canopy increases, even canopy you already had before the reduction."

"Even ground I already had."

"Even ground you already had," Pratt said, not unkindly, the way a person repeats a fact they've had to repeat often enough that the unkindness has worn off it for them, if it was ever there. "The review's on the acreage as proposed, not as it existed historically."

Ruth stood at the window a moment with both hands flat on the counter, the same way she'd stood over the packet at her own table, and understood that she had driven two hours and forty minutes each way and waited fifty for a fee schedule she could have priced closer to correctly sitting at home, and one new fact, which was that the ground she had cleared by hand in 2004 did not, in the eyes of this building, belong to her in any way that would be waiting for her if she ever gave it up.

"Is there anyone I can talk to about the timeline," she said. "Not the fee. The queue. How long before an inspector's actually out to my parcel."

Pratt told her that scheduling ran through a different office, second floor, and that Ruth would need a new number for that, and Ruth thanked her in the flat voice she used for weather reports and bad news alike, and took the envelope back, and got back in a different line.

The second floor was the room from the letter's description, more or less — fluorescent tube lighting, a laminated parcel map on the far wall color-coded by hand, most of it yellow, patches of red along the ridgelines, a scattering of green down near the valley floor where the flat, irrigated ground was. She sat another forty minutes. Somewhere behind the counter two staffers were talking, not loudly, not trying to be overheard, in the unguarded way people talk when they've stopped noticing the room has ears in it.

"— Canopy's whole packet again, the Higgins parcel addition. Complete on the first pass, if you can believe it. Consultant they use does this for a living, every exhibit numbered, cross-referenced, hydrology already stamped."

"Bump them up the sequence, then. No sense sitting a complete file behind six incomplete ones just because they came in later."

"Already did. Told Denise to slot it ahead of the Kettle Rock and Salmon Creek batch, those are all going to need three rounds of corrections apiece anyway, no reason to hold a clean file hostage to a messy one."

They moved on to something else, a form number, a printer that was low on toner, and neither of them looked toward the folding chairs, and neither of them would have recognized Kettle Rock Road as anything other than a line item if they had. Ruth sat with the fee schedule folded in quarters in her lap and did not move for a while. She had understood what she'd heard completely, and there was nothing to do with the understanding except carry it, the way she'd carried the packet's eleven pages the second time she read them, as a fact rather than a decision.

A clean file was not the same thing as a true one — she had built every foot of that terrace true to the land it sat on, true to what the land could carry without washing out, true in every sense a person who'd farmed it for forty years would use the word, and none of that had a box on a form, and the file that would beat hers into the county's queue by however many months was clean the way a spreadsheet is clean, prepared by a person paid to prepare it and paid again if it came back rejected, working from a parcel Ruth had never walked and never would.

Her number came up on the scheduling window not long after. The woman there — younger than Pratt, a different badge, a different flatness in the voice that Ruth had already come to recognize as the tone of the whole building rather than any one person's manner — told her that current wait for a compliance inspection ran fourteen to eighteen weeks from the date of a completed application, and that Ruth's file was not yet marked complete, since it lacked the engineered remediation plan for both flagged setback issues, which the county could not schedule around until it was submitted.

"So the clock doesn't start," Ruth said, "until I've already paid an engineer for a plan on a wall I don't know yet that I can afford to build."

"That's correct," the woman said, and did not apologize for it, because there was nothing in her job description that asked her to. Ruth thanked her the same way she'd thanked Pratt, folded the fee schedule in quarters again along its new crease, and left the ticket stub on the counter for whoever came after her.

She drove home with the fee schedule on the seat where the unopened envelope had ridden that morning, past Fortuna in the flat gray light that came before the fog burned off, past the turnoff for Redway where a white Canopy truck sat outside the IGA next to two flatbeds with plywood-patched sides, and did not stop, and did not turn the radio on.
