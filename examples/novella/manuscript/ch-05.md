Ruth found the water paperwork where she expected it, in the green filing box behind the propane tanks, though "paperwork" was generous for what was in the folder marked WATER in her mother's handwriting: a hand-drawn map of the intake from 1987, a receipt for eleven feet of four-inch PVC, and a single sheet, undated, on which her father had written the words *diversion approx 3 gpm, dry season* and nothing else. There was no Notice of Water Diversion and Use. There was no appropriative claim filed with the State Water Resources Control Board, no riparian documentation, no anything that a county reviewer would recognize as a legal basis for the six thousand gallons a day she pulled off the creek at the height of the season. She had known this in the way she knew the shed was thirty-eight feet from the boundary instead of fifty — as a fact she had simply never needed to look at — and she sat on the tailgate with the folder open on her knees and understood that of the four lines in Corinne Yee's report, this was the one that could not be fixed with a tape measure and a permit fee. This one required proving a right that had never existed on paper because for thirty-nine years no one had asked her to prove it.

She called the number the county had given her for a water rights consultant out of McKinleyville, a woman named Deb Ostrander who did this work for half the ridge, and Deb was kind and unhurried on the phone the way people were kind and unhurried with you right up until they told you the number, and the number, when it came, came in pieces: a forbearance agreement filing, an instream flow study if the creek was fish-bearing, which it was, a licensed hydrologist's report establishing pre-1914 use or an appropriative right in lieu of it, and if none of that could be established — and Deb said this gently, with the practiced patience of a woman who had read the same denial off State Water Board stationery more times than she cared to count — a new water right application that could take upward of two years to process and would almost certainly be denied for new appropriative diversions on a Class II watercourse during dry months regardless of how long she waited.

"So the storage tank," Ruth said. "If I go to storage and off-stream, winter fill only."

"That's what most people are doing," Deb said. "It's the only thing that pencils. You're looking at tankage, a new intake structure set back where you need it, the engineering on the whole system stamped, and my time on top of that. I could get you a number by Friday, but it isn't going to be a number you like."

Ruth thanked her and hung up and sat a while longer on the tailgate with the folder closed on her knees, looking down toward the creek she could not see from the yard but had listened to every day of her adult life, low now, the sound of it barely reaching the house this late in July, and thought that a woman with a rangefinder had been able to establish in an afternoon what it would take another woman with a hydrology license the better part of a year to unmake.

* * *

She heard the rest of it on Tuesday, in the parking lot of the feed store in Redway, from Hollis Meeker, who had run cattle and a mixed light-dep operation on the parcel two ridges over since before Ruth had planted her first mother plant, and who she trusted the way you trust someone you have disagreed with steadily and without malice for thirty years. He was loading a pallet of mineral blocks into his flatbed when she came out with a sack of chicken feed she didn't strictly need, and he lifted his chin at her the way he always did and said the county had finally signed off on the water for the Descanso parcels — the three that had gone to Canopy back in the spring, the old Whitmore ground and the two beside it.

"Six weeks," he said, hefting the last block up onto the bed. "Whitmore's kid was fighting that water claim with the county for four years on his own and got nowhere. Canopy comes in, brings their own hydrologist, their own engineer, files the whole package as one unit covering all three parcels, and six weeks later they've got a will-serve letter and a stamped plan. I heard it from Deb Ostrander's own mouth. She said she's never seen a packet move that clean."

"They'd have the money for it," Ruth said, careful to keep it flat.

"They'd have the staff for it," Hollis said. "That's the difference. Money buys you the staff, and the staff is what the county wants to see — somebody who fills the form out the way the form wants to be filled out the first time, instead of a rancher's kid trying to read the water code on his lunch break." He said it without particular bitterness, the way he said most things, the same even tone he'd use reading out a scale ticket. "You still fighting yours?"

"Working on it," Ruth said.

"Well." He shut the tailgate. "Good luck to you. I mean that."

She said thanks and meant it too, and they stood a moment longer in the heat off the asphalt not saying anything else, and then he got in his truck and she got in hers, and she drove the forty minutes home with the radio off, turning it over the way she'd turn a tool over in her hand to check it for a crack: it was not only that the county could not or would not help a woman like her get her water right in order. It was that the same county, presented with the same creek, the same statutes, the same fish-bearing Class II watercourse, had cleared three parcels for the one buyer capable of hiring the people who spoke the county's own language back to it, and had done so in six weeks flat, while Ruth's application sat behind an engineered remediation plan she could not yet afford to commission. It was not a broken system. A broken system would have failed everyone the same amount. This one worked exactly as built, and what it had been built to do, she understood now with a plainness that did not feel like anger so much as the settling of a scale, was to move land from people like her to people like Corinne Yee's employer, in an orderly fashion, with paperwork, and to call the orderliness fairness because no one had broken the rules to make it happen.

She did not go back to the compliance list that night. She fed the chickens, walked the rows with Wyatt while he checked the drip lines, said little, and after dark she sat at the kitchen table with the folder from the green box open in front of her — the hand-drawn map, the PVC receipt, her father's seven words about a diversion he had never once had to justify to anyone — and left the water line on the checklist blank, not because she had decided anything, but because for the first time since the permit application had arrived on her table she understood that filling it out correctly and filling it out in time were two different problems, and that only one of them was actually about the water.
