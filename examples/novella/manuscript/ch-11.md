Ruth woke at eleven to the sound of the barometer glass ticking against its brass housing on the mudroom wall, a sound she had trained herself to hear even asleep, the way a mother hears a change in a child's breathing. She lay still a moment, listening past it to the roof, and there it was: not the light patter the radio had promised at six but something heavier, wind-driven, coming in gusts that made the gutter downspout gurgle and choke. She was up and dressed before she let herself think about it in words.

The forecast had called for a tenth of an inch, maybe two, a system sliding south off the Gulf of Alaska and expected to break apart before it reached the county line. She had read it twice at the kitchen table that afternoon, the way she read everything now, and had decided against tarping the east block because the plants there were eleven days out from harvest and tarping cost airflow, and airflow was the only thing standing between a crop and botrytis once the rains started early. She had made the decision the way she had made ten thousand decisions like it, on judgment built from weather that had fooled her before and would fool her again, and she had gone to bed with the window cracked so she would hear if it changed its mind.

It had changed its mind.

She pulled on her boots without socks, grabbed the headlamp off its hook by the door, and went out into rain that was already an inch harder than anything the radio had described, cold and slanted, coming off the ridge in sheets that flattened the grass along the drive. Wyatt was in Willow Creek for the weekend, staying with a cousin near the job he had not yet taken, and she had told him to go, that August was quiet, that she could manage two days alone. She had believed it when she said it. Standing at the edge of the east block now with the beam of her headlamp cutting a cone through the rain, she believed it would have to be true whether she had meant it or not.

The colas on the eleven-day plants had gone dense in the last week the way they always did at the end, tight and heavy-headed, and it was exactly that density that made them vulnerable — moisture got in at the base of a swollen bud and had nowhere to go, and by morning what looked like a healthy plant could carry a gray rot working its way outward from the inside, invisible until she split a cola open and found it soft and brown at the core. There was no sensor for that. Canopy's people would come with moisture meters calibrated to soil, not flower, and even if they'd had the right instrument they would not have known, the way she knew, which cultivars ran that risk and which didn't, which rows sat low enough in the swale to pool water at the root crown, which plants she had watched every day for four months and could read now the way she read her own handwriting.

She worked the row by feel and headlamp, cupping each cola in her palm and pressing gently at the base, feeling for the give that meant water sitting where it shouldn't. Most of the plants she left alone. The ones that worried her — a run of six Skunk plants at the low end of the row, dense-flowered and eleven days too green to pull as a full harvest — she started cutting anyway, whole branches at a time, working the loppers by feel more than sight because the headlamp beam kept drowning in the rain. She hauled the branches under the drying barn eave in armloads, soaked to the shoulder within twenty minutes, and hung what she could on the lowest tier of rack, leaving space between each branch that she would not have left in a normal harvest, because tonight air moving through the rack mattered more than density on the line.

By one in the morning she had cut what needed cutting and turned to the rest of the block, and for those plants there was no cutting, only holding on until the storm passed or it didn't. She dragged the box fans from the trim shed out under the eave and ran extension cord through the rain to the panel, tying off the cord high on a post so it wouldn't sit in standing water, and pointed the fans down the row where she'd left the densest plants standing, not to dry them — nothing would dry them tonight — but to keep the air moving over the buds so water couldn't sit still long enough to become rot. It was a trick she had learned in 1998 from a woman named Alta Furse who was dead now eleven years, learned it the hard way after losing a third of an October harvest to a storm not much worse than this one, and she had never seen it written down anywhere, not in a county pamphlet, not in a cultivation manual, not on any of the forums Wyatt sometimes read her things from on his phone. It lived in her the way a phone number lives in a hand that has dialed it ten thousand times, and tonight it was the only tool she had that the rain couldn't take from her.

She worked until three, moving between the drying barn and the field, checking the low six plants she'd left standing, culling two more when her hand found the give she was looking for, running a extension line to a second set of fans when the wind shifted and started blowing straight down the row instead of across it. Twice she stopped, soaked through her rain gear, chest heaving, and thought about going in, and both times she thought instead of the number on Corinne Yee's report, seventeen thousand four hundred square feet of unpermittable full-sun canopy, and the number on Deb Ostrander's estimate, a hundred and seventy-one thousand dollars for a dam remediation she could not afford, and neither number was any use to her out here in the dark. Only her hands were any use to her out here in the dark. She kept working.

The rain broke a little after four, thinning to a drizzle and then to nothing, the way storms off that system usually did once they cleared the ridge, and by the time the sky over the Meekers' place had gone the color of ash the wind had dropped enough that she could hear the creek running high and loud below the south field. She walked the row one more time with the headlamp, checking every plant she'd left standing, and found the six she'd been most worried about had held, dense and heavy and wet but sound all the way through when she split a test cola open at the base to check, no soft brown place hiding inside, no smell of it starting. She stood there a long moment with the wet flower open in her palm under the beam of the headlamp, and did not feel triumph exactly, only the flat, physical certainty of a thing tested and found intact, the same feeling she imagined a mason might have running a hand along a wall the morning after an earthquake.

She did not call anyone. There was no one whose job it was to know that she had done this, no county form for it, no line item on Canopy's spreadsheet, no cell signal at four in the morning even if she had wanted to send Wyatt a text he wouldn't read until noon. She went inside, peeled off boots and rain gear that had stopped keeping water out an hour earlier, and stood dripping on the mudroom floor looking at the folder on the kitchen table, still sitting where it had sat for weeks, its checklist unfinished, its dam and its generator shed no closer to compliance than they had been the day before. Nothing about the folder had changed. She had changed, or rather some fact about her had been proven again that the folder could not touch one way or the other, and she went to bed at five with the sky graying outside the window and slept four hours without dreaming, the way she had slept after every hard night in forty years of hard nights, because the body, once it had done the work, no longer needed the mind's permission to rest.
