Ruth had learned, forty years ago, that a scale only told you the truth if you zeroed it first, and so on the Tuesday after Corinne Yee's report arrived she went out to the equipment shed before breakfast and did what she always did when a number was bothering her: she found something with a scale on it and used it.

The trim shears needed oiling. She had six pairs soaking in isopropyl in a coffee can, and she worked them one at a time, wiping each blade dry, dropping oil into the pivot, opening and closing the handles until the click came clean instead of gritty. It was work she could do with her eyes half shut. She had done it in the dark, in the rain, in a truck bed with a flashlight in her teeth while a deputy's spotlight swept the ridge above her in 1987, and her hands had not shaken then either. She set the last pair down on the bench, blades aligned, and did not pick up a seventh, because there wasn't one, and stood there a moment with her hands smelling of oil, not going back into the house.

The offer was still on the kitchen table where she had left it Saturday, weighted down with a jar of raw honey so the ceiling fan wouldn't lift the pages, and she had walked past it a dozen times since without opening it again, the way she'd once walked past a dog she didn't trust, keeping it in the corner of her eye. Corinne Yee's card was clipped to the front page. Nine thousand five hundred an acre for the raw parcel. Fourteen thousand if the application was active at closing. The offer held, the letter said, through the end of the month. She had read that line only once and it had been enough; she did not need to read it again to know it was still true, the way she did not need to check the sky twice to know it was going to rain.

Wyatt came at eight to bag soil, and she let him work the potting bench alone for the first time, standing at the door of the greenhouse to watch him measure amendment by scoop instead of eye the way she still did, counting under his breath, careful and slow and a little proud of himself for being trusted with it. She did not tell him what was sitting on her kitchen table. There was no reason he needed to carry it, not yet, not until she knew what shape it was going to take, and she found, watching him level a scoop of bat guano against the rim of a five-gallon bucket, that she wanted one more day of the operation being simply what it had always been: soil, water, light, a boy learning to measure things correctly.

That night she cleared the table.

She moved the honey jar, the seed catalogs, three months of unopened junk mail she'd been meaning to burn, and set down instead a legal pad, a pencil, and the calculator her father had used to balance the ledger books he'd kept in a coffee can under the floorboards, a machine with buttons worn smooth and a roll of paper long since gone that still, when she pressed the numbers in, gave her a green readout she trusted more than anything on a screen. She sat. She opened the report to the page with Corinne's figures typed in a clean sans-serif font that made even the bad news look tidy, and she began, the way she'd been taught to begin anything that mattered, with what she actually knew instead of what she was afraid of.

Fifty-eight acres. That was the parcel, the whole of it, deeded to her father in 1961 and to her in 1994, fence line to fence line, most of it timber and brush she had never once put a plant in. She wrote the number down and multiplied it by fourteen thousand, the higher figure, the one that assumed she got her application active in time, assumed Deb Ostrander's engineering plan came through, assumed the county moved for her the way it had moved for Hollis Meeker's neighbors in six clean weeks. Eight hundred and twelve thousand dollars. She looked at the sum a while. It was more money than she had ever had in a bank account at one time, more than the truck and the house and everything in the equipment shed put together, and she felt nothing about it at all, which surprised her enough that she wrote it again on a fresh line, as if a second time might produce a feeling the first time hadn't.

Instead she did what she had always done with a number that felt too large or too small to be true: she broke it down until it was a number she could hold in one hand.

Forty years. She wrote that under the total and divided, and the calculator gave her twenty thousand three hundred dollars, and change, per year, which was not nothing, she told herself, was in fact more than she had cleared most years in the eighties when the price per pound had been high enough to make a person careless and every dollar of it had to be laundered through a fence-painting business that painted very little fence. But a year was not a unit she trusted either, not for this, because a year held some weeks of harvest and some weeks of nothing and did not account for the difference between the two, and so she went further, the way she'd go further with a soil test that didn't look right, down past the number that satisfied a stranger to the number that would satisfy her.

She had worked, by her own rough count, something close to sixty hours a week for most of those forty years, more in July and August, less in the dead of winter when there was only pruning and planning and the books, and she wrote sixty times fifty weeks, allowing herself two weeks a year she hadn't actually taken, and got three thousand hours a year, and multiplied that by forty, and the calculator gave her a hundred and twenty thousand hours, which she looked at for a long moment because it was the first number all evening that felt like it might be roughly true.

Eight hundred twelve thousand divided by a hundred and twenty thousand came out, after she'd run it twice to be sure the machine hadn't slipped a digit, to six dollars and seventy-seven cents.

She sat with that.

It was less than she'd paid Wyatt that afternoon to weigh out potting soil. It was less, by a fair margin, than the minimum wage the state of California guaranteed to anyone bagging groceries or pumping gas, jobs that asked nothing of a person's hands beyond willingness, jobs she could have walked into at twenty-two and walked out of at retirement with a pension and a watch and never once had to learn what a Class II watercourse was or how to read a light cycle off the shape of a leaf or how to cut a clone so clean the wound never knew it had been cut. Six dollars and seventy-seven cents an hour was not an insult exactly, because an insult required someone to have looked at her and decided she was worth that little, and no one had looked at her at all. No person had set the number. An acreage figure had, and a market rate, and a formula that did not know, and had no column asking, what those hours had actually held — the way she'd learned to smell mold in a curing room before it showed on a single bud, the eleven years it had taken her to trim a cola so the resin stayed unbruised, the nights she'd sat up with a sick clone the way another woman might sit up with a fevered child, not because the plant was worth anything that week but because she had made a thing live and did not intend to let it die from her own inattention.

She set the pencil down.

The number was not wrong. That was the part she kept circling back to, turning it the way she'd turned Hollis Meeker's story over on the drive home from the feed store: eight hundred twelve thousand dollars was a real number, offered by a real company, backed by a signature she could put on a real piece of paper, and it would, if she took it, be more money at once than she had ever seen and would ever see again by any other means available to a woman her age with a bad knee and a business the county had just spent an afternoon proving didn't legally exist. The money measured a parcel — soil and slope, board feet of timber, square footage of full sun. What no form Corinne Yee's company had ever printed had a column for was the other thing, the thing that had actually taken forty years to make and could not be handed across a closing table with the acreage, because it did not live in the acreage. It lived, if it lived anywhere, in her hands, and in Wyatt's now, a little, if she kept teaching him right, and nowhere the deed recorded.

She did not sleep well. She lay in the dark doing arithmetic she had already finished doing, running the hundred and twenty thousand hours back up against the eight hundred twelve thousand dollars as if a different order of operations might change the answer, and toward two in the morning she gave up pretending sleep was coming and lay instead looking at the ceiling, listening to the house settle the way it had settled every night for thirty years, and understood with a plainness that felt almost like relief that the letter on her kitchen table was no longer something she could set the honey jar on and walk past. It had a number in it now that she herself had made, worse than the one Corinne had brought, and it would sit there tomorrow and the day after, gathering the small daily weight of a thing that has to be answered, whether or not she ever opened the folder again to look at it.
