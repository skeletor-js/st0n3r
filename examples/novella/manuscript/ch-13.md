The last week of harvest fell hard against the calendar Ruth had been trying not to look at, the surveyor's date circled in one corner of it and the county hearing notice pinned in the other, so that by the second week of October she had stopped consulting it at all and gone instead by the plants, the way she always had, waiting for the fan leaves to yellow at the base and the pistils to curl back like something withdrawing from the light. She walked the rows at dawn with a loupe in her shirt pocket and checked the trichomes on the cola tips in three spots per plant, milky heads gone mostly cloudy with a scatter of amber starting in, and by the time she reached the end of the fourth row she had made the call the way she made it every year: today, this block first, before the fog burned off and softened the resin.

Wyatt came at seven with the truck and they worked the first two rows together, him on the loppers cutting the main stalks at the base while she followed behind trimming the fan leaves close and hanging the branches upside down on the drying lines strung under the barn eaves, six inches of space between each one so the air could move through. He had gotten faster over the season without losing the care she'd drilled into him in June, cutting clean at an angle so the stem wouldn't crush, and she told him so once, plainly, the way she told him anything that was true, and he nodded and kept working.

At ten he said he had to leave by noon, an orientation session in Willow Creek, paperwork and a tour, nothing that would take long, and she said that was fine and did not ask him to explain further, because there was nothing in it that needed explaining. He had told her about the job three weeks earlier, in the same flat voice he used for everything, and she had said good, meaning it, and neither of them had brought it up since. He worked through the rest of the morning at the same pace as always, and when he left at noon he carried two full trays to the barn first without being asked, and she watched him go down the drive in his own truck and then turned back to the block, alone now, with maybe a third of the harvest still standing.

She worked through the afternoon by herself, cutting and carrying and hanging, the barn filling row by row with drying branches until the air under the eaves smelled the way it smelled every October, green and sharp and turning, a smell she had never once written a note about because there was nothing to write, it simply told her what it told her: too fast and the chlorophyll would cure into the smoke, too slow and she'd get mold in the thick colas before the stems ever snapped dry. She adjusted two of the barn fans by feel, cracked the north door six inches instead of the usual four because the humidity had come up with the last of the fog, and did not think of it as decision-making so much as listening, which was what it had always been.

It was somewhere in the sixth row, hauling a cut branch heavy with buds toward the truck bed, that she found herself doing the arithmetic again without having meant to start it — fourteen thousand an acre, fifty-eight acres, minus what the shed and the dam would cost her if she carried the fight herself, minus nothing at all if she signed and let Canopy carry it instead, plus whatever the land might still be worth to her in a form no ledger recognized — and she noticed, hauling the branch, that the number had stopped moving her the way it had in June. It sat in her the way a fact sits once you've turned it over enough times: flat, known, no longer capable of surprise. She set the branch down on the truck bed and did not pick the arithmetic back up. She went back for the next one instead.

By late afternoon the fog had not burned off at all but thickened, and she cut faster through the last standing plants, watching the sky the way she'd watched it the night of the storm, not with fear exactly but with the old attention that had kept things alive on this ground for forty years. The light went gray and stayed gray, and she worked the last row in it, and it was there, stripping fan leaves from a branch with her thumb and forefinger in the motion she could do without looking, that she understood — not decided, the deciding had apparently already happened somewhere she hadn't been watching — that she was not going to sign the purchase agreement.

She did not stop moving when she understood it. She stripped the branch clean, hung it on the line, and reached for the next one, and if there was a feeling attached to the understanding she did not stay with it long enough to name it, only noted, the way she'd note a change in wind, that the folder on the kitchen table had become a formality now rather than a question, and that she would have to call Corinne Yee before the week was out and say so, and that there would be consequences to it she had not yet let herself add up: the dam, the shed, the water rights fight, the hearing in three weeks that could end the whole thing anyway without her ever having to make a choice at all. None of that moved through her hands. Her hands kept doing what they were doing, which was hanging the last plant of the sixth row and starting on the seventh.

She finished the block after dark, working by the yard light and a headlamp for the final hour, and drove the last full trays to the barn a little after nine. The barn held the whole crop now, close to four hundred plants strung along the lines in the dark, dripping slightly onto the plastic sheeting she'd laid down that morning, and she stood in the doorway a moment with the headlamp off, letting her eyes adjust, listening to the fans and the drip and the particular silence of a barn full of curing plants, which was not really silence at all but the sound of forty years doing what it knew how to do without her standing over it.

She did not call Corinne Yee that night. She went inside, ate standing at the counter, and left the folder exactly where it had sat for weeks, unopened, the price still unsigned inside it. In the days that followed she finished the harvest in the north and east blocks, working with Wyatt on the mornings he was free and alone on the afternoons he wasn't, moving through October the way she had moved through every October since 1985 — cutting, hanging, adjusting the barn fans by smell and feel, turning branches that were drying unevenly, pulling the ones that were ready and leaving the ones that weren't — until the last plant came down on the twenty-second, and the whole crop hung curing in the dark, and the compliance folder sat on the kitchen table exactly as it had before, holding a decision she had already made and had not yet told anyone she'd made at all.
