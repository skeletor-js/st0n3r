Ruth spent Tuesday morning doing work that would not matter.

She swept the concrete pad in front of the generator shed with a push broom, though there was nothing on it but a summer's worth of fir needles and the rust-colored dust that settled on everything within thirty feet of the road. She coiled the black poly hose that ran loose from the second greenhouse and hung it on its nail instead of leaving it in its usual sprawl across the path. She walked the tree line above the drying shed and dragged two fallen limbs clear of the door, so the building read as tended rather than merely used. None of it changed a single number on the parcel. She understood this while she was doing it, understood it the way she understood that raking gravel over a mud patch didn't fix the drainage underneath, and did it anyway, because a woman with thirty years of a place in her hands could not walk into an inspection of that place with her hands empty.

Wyatt found her at it a little after eight, hosing down the pad a second time though it was already clean, and came to lean against the corner of the shed with a nursery tray under one arm, watching her the way he watched anything he hadn't been told the shape of yet.

"Corinne coming today?"

"Eleven." Ruth shut off the hose. "You keep on the west row. She's not here for you."

"She here for the county?"

"She's here for Canopy." Ruth wound the hose back onto its nail, a second time, more carefully than the first. "Not the same thing. Feels like it, some days."

He didn't ask what the difference was, and she didn't explain it, because the honest answer was that she'd spent four days that week telling herself there was one, and Thursday's mail — the county application still open on the kitchen table, corrective costs still unpriced — had not made the case any easier to hold.

Corinne Yee arrived at eleven exactly, in a gray crew-cab with the Canopy leaf small and green on the door, and came up the spur road on foot from where she'd parked rather than driving it, which Ruth noted and did not comment on. She wore boots that had clearly been bought for this kind of visit and not worn on one before, and she carried a tablet in a rubberized case and a laser rangefinder no bigger than a garage-door opener, and she shook Ruth's hand once, firmly, the grip of someone who had practiced not letting a handshake read as either weak or performed.

"I appreciate you making time for this," Corinne said. "I know a site visit isn't necessarily how you'd choose to spend a Tuesday."

"I don't choose much about my Tuesdays anymore."

Corinne smiled at that in a way that suggested she'd file it somewhere, and Ruth understood, watching her do it, that the smile cost her nothing and meant nothing and was simply what her face did while her mind moved on to the next line of the visit.

They walked the terrace first. Corinne asked competent questions about the greenhouses — build year, framing, poly replacement schedule — and typed the answers without looking down at the screen, the way a woman drives a road she's driven before. She did not linger over the mother plants, though her eyes went to them twice, and she did not ask about the pruning method Ruth had spent June teaching Wyatt, though Ruth had the sense, watching her, that Corinne Yee could smell forty years of unwritten knowledge on a place the way a buyer smells water damage under new paint — present, unlisted, and worth something to somebody, just not to the form she carried.

At the full-sun plot Corinne stopped and made a note that took longer than the others.

"This whole four-tenths of an acre is showing as uncured Category Two under the current file," she said, not looking up. "Per the current schedule, full-sun isn't permittable at all until the shed setback and the water items clear. I want to flag that for you now, not because it changes anything today, but because I don't think anyone at the county's walked you through it in plain terms."

"They told me plainly enough."

"Then I'll say it again. It's material to the number I bring back."

They went on to the generator shed last, downhill and east of the greenhouses, where the diesel smell hung the way it always did after a morning fill, and Corinne crouched at the shed's east wall and set the rangefinder against the siding and shot a line to the survey stake at the old Ostrander boundary, the same iron pin Ruth's father had driven in 1971 and that Ruth had used for every measurement she'd ever made on this land without once needing a number to go with it.

The rangefinder read out in red digits Ruth could see from where she was standing. Thirty-eight feet. It was the same figure she had carried since 1987, when she'd walked this ground with her father's stakes and chosen the spot herself, and there had never been any question in her mind of what the number was, only of what it would come to mean once someone else had cause to read it back to her. Now someone had.

Corinne looked at the number, looked once at the shed, and wrote it down.

"County minimum's fifty," she said. "Per code." It wasn't a question and she didn't ask Ruth to confirm it, and there was nothing in her voice when she said it — no correction, no sympathy, no surprise, the flat tone of a woman reading a thermometer. She typed the figure into the tablet and moved on to the dam without waiting to see what the number did to Ruth's face, which was, Ruth thought later, its own kind of mercy, or its own kind of practice.

The intake dam took longer. Corinne walked the bank above it, shot the line twice to be sure, and got seventy-four feet from the top of bank to the diversion structure against a required hundred, six feet short of the eighty Ruth had always carried in her head for that stretch of bank, and lingered a moment with the tributary moving low and clear beneath her before she wrote that number down too, without comment, the same as the first.

Ruth had built that dam by hand in 1987 with a man named Carl Jespersen who was dead now eleven years, had sited it exactly where the creek narrowed enough to hold a head of water and exactly far enough upstream from the trail that nobody walking the ridge would hear it running, and had never once, in thirty-nine years, needed a tape to tell her it was in the right place. It had been in the right place. It still was, for every purpose she had ever asked of it. The problem was that it had been sited for a world that measured things by whether they worked, and it was being read now by a world that measured them by distance from a line that hadn't existed when Carl Jespersen had his boots in that water.

They finished at the drying shed, where Corinne asked about the irrigation lines — where the poly ran, whether any of it was mapped — and Ruth said no, none of it, that she carried the whole system in her head the way she carried the property lines, and Corinne wrote *undocumented distribution, no as-built* without any change in her face at all.

They ended back at the top of the spur road where Corinne had parked, and Corinne closed the tablet's case, which seemed to be a habit she performed at the close of every visit rather than a decision she made about this one.

"I want to be transparent with you, Ruth. I don't think you're someone who responds well to soft-pedaling." She said it without any particular warmth and without any particular coldness, a simple statement of method. "Our standing offer on a raw parcel this size is ninety-five hundred an acre, per the current schedule. If your application's still active and formally filed at close — which yours is — that number steps up to fourteen thousand, and Canopy assumes the remainder of the corrective action list at that point. The shed. The dam. All of it. You wouldn't need to touch any of it again."

"And the genetics."

"Genetics aren't a line item in the appraisal. I'm aware of that." For the first time since she'd arrived, something moved under the flatness of her voice, brief enough that Ruth might have imagined it. "I understand what forty years of a mother plant line represents to the person who kept it going. That's just not a figure I'm authorized to assign."

"No," Ruth said. "You're not."

Corinne didn't argue the point, which was, in its way, more unsettling than if she had. She said she'd have the site notes typed into a formal list and sent that afternoon, so Ruth would have the county language in hand and not just what she remembered from the walk, and she promised it the way she might have promised to mail a receipt, and then she got in the gray truck and backed it down to the county road without once looking back up the spur, and was gone.

Ruth lingered at the top of the road a while after the sound of the truck had thinned out into the ordinary sound of the afternoon — a jay somewhere in the firs, the generator's low idle, Wyatt's shears clicking two rows over, steady and unhurried, a man who had no idea what had just been measured forty yards from where he worked.

The email came at 4:52. Ruth read it at the kitchen counter with her boots still on, the way she'd read the permit application the week before, and then she carried her laptop to the printer in the hall closet, the old inkjet that jammed if you loaded more than a dozen sheets, and fed it paper one small stack at a time and waited while it worked, page by page, a low mechanical drone that filled the house the way nothing in that house had filled it in a long time.

Generator shed, 38 ft from parcel boundary; setback required, 50 ft. Non-conforming.
Intake structure, 74 ft from top of bank of Class II watercourse; setback required, 100 ft. Non-conforming.
Irrigation distribution system: no as-built documentation on file. Required prior to final.
Full-sun cultivation area, approx. 17,400 sq ft: not permittable under current corrective action status.

Four lines. She had expected more, somehow, though there was no reason four lines should have felt like less than eleven pages. It was only that the eleven pages had been the law describing what a compliant parcel looked like in the abstract, a document that could have been about anyone's land or no one's, and these four lines were about this land, measured from this stake to this wall by a woman standing where Ruth had stood a thousand times without ever once needing to know the number.

She read it through twice, the way she'd read the application, and then she did not sit down right away. She held the pages and looked out the kitchen window toward the spur road, where the fog had already begun coming up cool off the creek the way it did most evenings by six, and understood, without any particular drama attached to the understanding, that whatever she decided to do about any of it — fight the setback, sell the parcel, let the whole thing sit unresolved through another winter — the seeing itself could not be undone. Somebody with a rangefinder and a tablet had come to the edge of her father's boundary stake and turned thirty-nine years of careful placement into four lines of noncompliance, and the lines would exist now whether or not she ever signed anything, filed anything, or spoke to Corinne Yee again.

She set the pages on the table next to the still-unfinished permit application, squared the corners the way she squared everything, and went out to help Wyatt close down the irrigation for the night.
