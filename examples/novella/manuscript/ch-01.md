Ruth cut the clone at a forty-five degree angle, just below a node, the way she had been cutting them since 1986, and set it root end up in the tray of rockwool cubes before the cut had time to seal itself over with air. Forty cuts an hour if she didn't talk. Thirty if she did. She had done the math so many springs ago that it no longer felt like arithmetic, just a fact about her hands, like their reach or their grip strength, both of which had also been measured over the years, mostly by what they could no longer do.

The greenhouse smelled of rooting gel and wet perlite and, underneath that, of the diesel that never quite left the air near the generator shed no matter which way the wind came off the ridge. Fog sat in the trees below the terrace, waiting on the sun. She had four hundred and twenty mother plants to take cuttings from before noon, and she meant to have every one of them stuck and misted and under the domes by the time the fog burned off, because that was the schedule, and the schedule did not care what else had arrived in the mail.

Wyatt worked the tray beside hers, slower, watching her hands more than his own. He had been on the parcel three weeks. He cut a stem too high, an inch above the node instead of just below it, and held it up without being asked, the cut end toward her like a question.

"Too much wood in that one," she said. "It'll root, but it'll take twice as long and give you a weaker plant. Cut close. The node's where the hormone wants to work from."

He nodded and didn't argue and didn't apologize either, which she had noted about him the first week and filed away as the single most useful thing she knew about him so far. He set the bad cutting in a separate cup instead of the tray, to root anyway and see, and went back to work.

"You're not going to throw it out," she said.

"No point wasting it if it might take."

She let that stand. It was a small thing, an inch of stem, but she had spent forty years noticing which employees discarded a thing because it was wrong by the book and which ones kept it because it might still be right in fact.

The packet had come Tuesday. It was Thursday now, and it still sat on the kitchen table where she'd set it down after carrying it in from the mailbox, weighted at one corner by the salt shaker so the wind through the screen door wouldn't take it. HUMBOLDT COUNTY PLANNING AND BUILDING DEPARTMENT, it said, in the upper left corner, over a return address in Eureka. She had not opened it. She had walked past it nine or ten times since Tuesday — to get her boots, to get coffee, to answer the phone — and each time some part of her attention had gone to it the way an animal's attention goes to a sound it can't yet place, and each time she had let the rest of her keep walking.

They worked through the morning without talking much, which suited her. By ten the fog had burned off the way it always did, and the light came down flat and full onto the two houses of blue poly tarp, sun-faded on the south side, patched on the north with a newer, darker blue where a storm had torn a seam two winters back. Four hundred cuttings in, she stopped to eat a sandwich standing at the potting bench, and Wyatt asked her, not for the first time, why she still ran two separate mother rooms instead of consolidating into one.

"Genetics get confused if you crowd them," she said. "You start taking cuts off a plant that's stressed from being next to something it doesn't like, you don't always see it right away. Takes a season sometimes before the clone shows you what the mother was carrying."

"How do you know if a mother's carrying something, before you see it in the clone?"

"You don't, always. That's why you keep two rooms. So if one goes bad you haven't lost the whole line." She looked at him a beat longer than the answer required. "You ask good questions."

He took that the way he took most things she gave him, without much visible change in his face, and went back to the tray. She thought, not for the first time that week, that he asked the kind of questions a person asked when they wanted to be taught something rather than just told it, and that this was rarer in hired help than people would guess, and that she hadn't yet decided how much of the rest of it — the trellising, the timing on topping, the two things she had never written down anywhere because writing them down had never once been asked of her — she meant to give him. There would be time to decide. There was always more season left than there seemed to be, until suddenly there wasn't.

By four they had eleven hundred cuttings under the humidity domes, misted and labeled by mother number in a grease pencil that would smear if she wasn't careful walking past with a wet sleeve. She sent Wyatt down to the house to get the propagation mats plugged in and warming before the evening chill came up off the creek, and stayed another twenty minutes herself, walking the rows of mothers, running a thumb along a leaf here and there, checking for the faint stipple that meant spider mite before it was bad enough to see from three feet off. She had learned to read a leaf the way some people read a face. Neither skill had ever once been asked for on a form.

The packet was still on the table when she came in to start dinner. Wyatt had eaten early and gone back to his trailer at the far end of the spur, the way he did most nights, and she was alone in the kitchen with the sound of the propagation mats humming faintly through the wall and the packet sitting there under the salt shaker like something she had agreed to board for a friend and was waiting to be asked to give back.

She made herself a plate of eggs and the last of a loaf of bread going stale, and ate standing at the counter rather than sitting across from it, and told herself this was because she was tired, which was true, and not because sitting down across the table from the packet felt like something she wasn't ready to do, which was also true.

There was a second stack of paper on the far end of the table, smaller, that she had opened three days before it — Corinne Yee's letter, on Canopy letterhead, laying out again in slightly warmer language than the first time what the company was prepared to offer for the parcel, the water right they already effectively controlled downstream, and Ruth's forty years of knowing exactly how to grow the thing they wanted to grow at scale without knowing any of it themselves. That letter she had read the day it came. It had not frightened her the way the county packet, unopened, now did, and she had turned that fact over more than once without landing anywhere useful. A woman with a checkbook was at least a thing she understood. A government envelope was not new to her, not after forty years of understanding exactly how much of her life had been arranged around staying out of one, but this one had her name on it in a way none of the others ever had, sent to her, inviting her in, and that was the part she didn't have a category for yet.

She washed the plate. She wiped the counter down twice, though it hadn't needed a second pass. She stood at the sink long enough to watch the last of the light go off the ridge above the drying shed, orange going to gray going to the flat no-color of full dark, and somewhere in that stretch of minutes she stopped finding reasons to be doing something else.

She sat down and pulled the packet toward her and opened it, and then, before she'd read a full line, she stood back up, because it was easier to read standing, the way she'd always preferred to read anything that might require her to move fast afterward.

The cover letter was two paragraphs and told her nothing she didn't already know: that the county's cultivation ordinance required an active permit application on file, that inspection would follow, that noncompliance would be sorted by category and cured or it would not be. She set that page aside. The rest of it ran to eleven pages, a checklist with boxes down the left margin, and she read it the way she read a weather report before a harvest, looking not for the general shape of the thing but for the specific number that would tell her whether she was in trouble.

Structure setbacks: fifty feet from any parcel line, cultivation or accessory. She thought of the generator shed, thirty-eight feet from the old Ostrander line, and did not need to get a tape measure to know it, because she had walked that ground with her father's boundary stakes in 1987 and had chosen the spot herself, for the fold in the land that swallowed the engine noise, not for a distance that wouldn't exist as a rule for another thirty years.

One hundred feet from the top of bank of any Class I or Class II watercourse. The intake dam — seventy-four feet from the tributary, by her own rough count, and she had never once had reason to count it before tonight.

Water diversion: any draw off a Class I or II stream had to cease June first through October thirty-first, the exact five months her intake dam had drawn water every year since 1987, because those were the five months anything needed watering. No pre-1914 claim. No riparian filing. She had never filed for one because for thirty years there had been no one to file with, and by the time there was, the reach below her was already spoken for — she had heard, secondhand, the way she heard most things about the neighboring parcel now, that Canopy held the only certificated right on that stretch of the creek, which meant there was no door left open to walk through late.

Thirty thousand gallons of storage, filled through the winter months, to get around the forbearance window. She did the arithmetic on the tank cost from the number printed at the bottom of that section almost without meaning to, the way she'd have priced out fencing or fuel, and set the total down next to the number Corinne Yee's letter had offered her for the whole parcel, and made herself stop before the comparison went any further than that.

An RFID tag for every plant over eight inches, logged at wet weight, dry weight, and trim weight, tracked through a state system she had heard of only in the flat, distant way she'd heard of things that happened to other people's operations, and a note in smaller print about the cell service required to run it, which she already knew, without needing the packet to tell her, did not reliably reach her kitchen table, let alone the terrace.

Violation categories. Category One, paperwork, thirty days, no cost. Category Two, setback or water source or structural, sixty days, twelve hundred dollars to reinspect, plus an engineered plan for whatever she meant to do about it. Category Three, repeat findings, any discharge to a watershed — cultivation suspended, a hearing, the permit itself revocable, and she read that word twice standing at the table with the lamp throwing her shadow long across the linoleum, because it was the only word in the eleven pages that didn't leave her a next step.

A reduced license, at the bottom, for growers who couldn't cure it all in time. Five thousand square feet, wherever the county decided her compliant ground was, out of the twenty-three thousand four hundred she had built by hand over eighteen years, one greenhouse and then a second and then the full-sun plot, each one placed exactly where the land let her hide it and none of them placed where a line on a county map would one day say they were allowed to be. No full-sun cultivation at all, the page said, on any parcel still carrying an uncured Category Two finding, and she had two right there in black and white before she'd even finished reading, the shed and the water, each one older than the ordinance that now measured her by it.

She read all eleven pages again, standing, in the same order, the way she'd once read a bud on the scale twice before she trusted the number, because a mistake read once could still be a mistake and a mistake read twice, if it came out the same both times, was just a fact she now had to carry.

Then she sat down. The chair took her weight the way it had for thirty years, an old kitchen chair with a wobble in the back left leg she had never fixed because she liked knowing exactly how far she could lean before it complained. She put both hands flat on the table on either side of the packet, not moving it, and looked at it a while longer, the way she might have looked at a frost warning two weeks before a harvest.
