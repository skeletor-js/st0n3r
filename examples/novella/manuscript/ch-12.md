Corinne Yee's office in Willow Creek smelled of new carpet and the particular ozone smell of a photocopier that had been running all morning, and Ruth sat in a chair across a conference table from a man she had not met before, introduced as Canopy's regional acquisitions counsel, a name she forgot within the minute of hearing it and did not ask to have repeated. Corinne sat beside him with a laptop open and a stack of paper turned face-down, the way a dealer turns cards before the hand starts, and Ruth understood before anyone spoke that this was not the meeting she had driven forty minutes expecting, the one where she said yes or no to $14,000 an acre and they shook hands and it was over.

"We've revised the offer," Corinne said, and turned the stack over, and slid it across the table.

The purchase price had not moved. Fourteen thousand an acre, application active at closing, Canopy assuming the remediation costs on the dam and the shed, the same numbers Ruth had carried in her head since the day of the site assessment, since Corinne had stood in the south field with a rangefinder and told her the distance from the intake to the watercourse in feet and tenths of a foot. What was new sat on page four, under a heading in bold that read CULTIVATION CONSULTING AGREEMENT, and Ruth read it once standing at the edge of the table without sitting down, the way she had read the county checklist in her kitchen in June, because some part of her had learned that certain documents needed to be read on her feet before she could trust herself to sit with them.

The clause offered her a contract: two years, renewable, forty thousand dollars a year, in exchange for what it called, in language a lawyer had clearly built out of some other document and dropped in whole, the documentation, transfer, and training delivery of Grower's proprietary cultivation methodologies, including but not limited to pruning, training, curing, and pest and disease management techniques developed or refined by Grower over the course of Grower's operation. There was a line for her to initial acknowledging that all such methodologies, once documented under the agreement, would become the sole intellectual property of Canopy Holdings LLC, its successors and assigns. There was a second line specifying that documentation would take the form of written protocols and recorded instructional video, to be produced according to a schedule set by Canopy's cultivation training division, for use across Canopy's operations statewide.

"We'd want you filming by September," the lawyer said. "Corinne tells us you're something of a — " he glanced at his own paper, as if the word were written there and he did not fully trust himself to produce it unassisted — "a specialist. In pruning technique specifically. That's a skill set we don't currently have documented anywhere in our training materials."

Ruth did not sit down.

She thought of the low tunnel behind Alta Furse's barn in 1998, the two of them on their knees in the mud with a flashlight held in Ruth's teeth, Alta's hands moving over a plant showing early signs of bud rot, splitting stems that looked healthy from the outside to show her the brown starting at the core, and Alta saying, not unkindly, that this was not a thing you could learn from a book because the book would tell you what rot looked like and not what it felt like a week before it started, the particular give in the stem, and that Ruth would have to grow that feeling into her hands herself over years, the way Alta had, and that once she had it she should be careful who she gave it to, because there was a difference between teaching a person and handing a person something that had cost you a decade. The intake dam remediation ran a hundred and seventy-one thousand dollars, three years of her best income, and forty thousand a year, for two years, came close enough to closing that gap that she made herself hold the thought a moment before she let it go.

"How would the filming work," she said, "exactly."

"A crew comes out," Corinne said. "You walk them through a plant. Full explanation, camera on your hands, on the plant, whatever angle they need. We'd want the botrytis management piece especially — that's come up as a real gap this year, given the weather. Storm risk, what to do in the first six hours. That kind of institutional knowledge is exactly what this clause is built to capture."

Ruth looked at the word institutional and thought of herself at three in the morning ten days ago, soaked through, running an extension cord to a second fan by headlamp with rain coming sideways off the ridge, testing a stem's give with her thumb the way her hand had learned to do it in a hundred storms before this one, no camera anywhere near her, no crew, no schedule set by anyone's training division, only the rain and the dark and the plant and forty years of her hand knowing what it was feeling for. That knowledge had never once, in forty years, needed anyone's permission to exist. It had lived in her hands the way nothing in this room could live — not the fluorescent tube humming overhead, not the cursor blinking on the lawyer's laptop, not the stack of paper Corinne could turn face-down and deal out again whenever she chose — and it had never occurred to her, not once, not even the July afternoon she'd finally shown Wyatt the cut, that the knowledge itself was a thing that could be owned by someone standing outside the row.

"No," she said.

The lawyer looked up from his laptop.

"The consulting piece," Ruth said. "Not the rest. I'm not saying no to the land, I haven't decided that, and I'm not asking you to change the price. I'm telling you I won't sign the part where you own what's in my hands." She sat down finally, and slid page four back across the table without the others, keeping the purchase agreement in front of her, squaring its edges the way she squared a stack of trim trays at the end of a long day, a small motion that had nothing to do with the papers and everything to do with giving her hands something to do that was not shaking. "You can have the ground. You can have the water rights fight and the shed and the dam, if I decide to give you those, and I understand what that costs me, I've done the arithmetic on it more times than you'd believe. But there isn't a form for the other thing. I spent thirty years learning to feel rot in a stem before it showed, and I'm not going to stand in front of a camera crew in September and hand that to a training division so it can get filed next to a video about trellis height. If I teach it, I'll teach it to a person. Not a protocol."

Corinne did not argue the point, which told Ruth something about how much weight the consulting clause had actually carried on Canopy's side of the table — a sweetener, not the deal itself, easily withdrawn, already half-forgotten by the time Corinne slid page four into a folder without comment and made a note in the margin of her own copy. The lawyer said something about revising the paperwork and having a clean version to her by the end of the week, and Ruth said that was fine, and stood, and shook both their hands, and drove home with the windows down through Willow Creek and out along the river road with the purchase price still sitting where it had sat for weeks, unsigned, and one clause gone from the table that she had not known, until she'd read it standing up, she would ever have needed to refuse.

She did not tell Wyatt about the meeting. She did not write to Iris. She put the folder back on the kitchen table when she got home, the price still open, the deadline still the end of the month, and went out to check the six plants she'd saved in the storm, moving down the row with her hands doing what they had always done, and did not think of the number on any page, only of the give of a stem under her thumb, which remained, after everything, entirely hers to feel and no one else's to keep.

She did not decide that night, but she stopped treating the folder like a thing that could simply wait for someone else's clock. Before she went to bed she found the old fifty-foot tape in the shop and walked out into the dark with a flashlight to measure the setback herself, from the shed wall to the property line and back again, twice, writing the number down on the back of an envelope so that whatever she signed, if she signed anything, would carry her own arithmetic under it and not only Canopy's. She left the purchase agreement open on the kitchen table overnight for the first time since June.
