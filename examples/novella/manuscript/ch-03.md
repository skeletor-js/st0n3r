Saturday morning Ruth found Wyatt already at the second greenhouse when she came down from the house, standing at the near end of the row with his hands loose at his sides, waiting for her rather than starting without her, which she had noticed he always did and had not yet decided what to make of.

"You're early," she said.

"Figured Saturday you'd want more done before it got hot."

It was not going to get hot. The fog hadn't burned off the ridge yet and wouldn't for another hour, and the greenhouse held the cool of the night inside its plastic the way it always did until midmorning, but she let the reasoning stand, because it was the kind of wrong guess that came from paying attention rather than from guessing, and those she'd learned to tell apart in three weeks of watching him.

"We're topping today," she said. "The whole east row, then we'll see about the west if there's light left in the afternoon."

He nodded and reached for the shears on the bench, the clean pair, not the ones with tape wound around the handle that she kept for herself, and she watched him check the blades against his thumbnail before he moved, a habit nobody had taught him this season because it wasn't a habit anybody needed to be taught twice.

She started them both at opposite ends of the row so the work would meet in the middle, and for a while there was only the sound of it — the small give of a stem under the blade, the shift of leaves as a hand moved to the next plant, her own breath going shallow the way it did when she was counting nodes without meaning to count them out loud. She had been topping plants since she was twenty-one and her hands did it now the way they poured coffee, without asking her opinion first, cutting each plant just above the fourth node so it would split into two colas instead of one, a decision that in a good year meant the difference between a plant that filled its space and one that wasted it reaching straight up into nothing.

Wyatt was cutting at the third node.

She saw it four plants into his side of the row and didn't say anything at first, because a wrong cut at the third node instead of the fourth wasn't ruinous — the plant would still split, would still yield, just narrower and later than it should, a difference nobody looking at the greenhouse in October would be able to name even if they felt it in the numbers — and there was a version of this morning where she let it go and corrected the ledger in her head and said nothing, because saying something meant explaining why the fourth node and not the third, and explaining that meant explaining the shape a plant took when it had four extra days of vegetative growth banked into the split versus three, which meant explaining light penetration into a canopy that hadn't budded yet, which was forty minutes of a conversation that would only matter to somebody who intended to still be standing in this greenhouse in five years asking the same kind of question unprompted.

She found her hand had already gone still at her side, the shears closed, waiting for him to reach the end of his row so she could switch out his plants for the ones she'd already done and nobody would ever know the difference. It was not a decision so much as the absence of one. She noticed it the way she noticed weather — after the fact, once it had already started raining.

"Wyatt."

He looked up.

"Show me your last cut."

He held up the stem, four inches of it curled loose in his palm where he'd already thrown the trim, and she took it and turned it so the cut face caught what light there was.

"Third node," she said. "Not fourth."

"I counted from the top."

"Count from the base. Every time. Top node's not reliable this early, they don't always show yet, especially on the runts toward the end of a row." She set the stem down on the bench rather than the compost bucket, which she noticed him notice. "Fourth node splits you two colas with four more days of veg banked into them than the third does. Come October that's the difference between a canopy that closes over itself and one that's still got gaps a hawk could fly through."

"Why's that matter, the gaps?"

"Light gets down into bud sites that should've stayed shaded. Buds that grow in light that isn't meant for them come out loose. Airy. Buyer weighs the jar and it's lighter than it looks, and there's no fixing that in the trim, you're just trimming what you already lost."

He was quiet a second, working it, and then he said, "So it's not really about the cola splitting even. It's about what happens six weeks after that, that you're setting up now."

"That's the whole job," she said. "Everything you do to a plant in June you're doing for what it needs in September. Most of growing's like that. You're never really working on the day you're standing in."

He went back to the row and started counting from the base, out loud at first, under his breath, until she told him to stop saying it and just do it, because a grower who needed to say the count out loud in June would still need to say it out loud in six years, and the ones worth their wage stopped needing to hear themselves think.

They worked through the row without talking much after that, and she let herself watch him longer than the job required, the way his weight settled into a stance before he made a cut instead of reaching in off-balance, the way he'd started, three weeks in, to touch a leaf before he touched the stem beneath it, checking color and turgor without being told that was a thing worth checking. None of it was hers. He'd brought most of it from wherever his family's operation had been, some version of this same knowledge learned on some other hillside from somebody who wasn't her, and what she was doing wasn't teaching him to grow. It was teaching him to grow this ground, her ground, the particular light and drainage and wind of it, which was a smaller thing and took longer.

Halfway down the row her hand stopped again.

There was a cut past the topping, one she used maybe twice a season on a plant that had grown lopsided from a light leak or a bent stake, a way of pruning the dominant cola back hard enough to force the plant to redistribute its energy into the weaker side without shocking it into stalling for a week, and she had never shown it to anyone, not in forty years, not because it was a secret worth keeping from the county or from a buyer but because it had taken her eleven seasons of getting it wrong — of stalling plants, of losing whole colas to a cut made a day too late or too early — to get it right, and there had never before now been a reason to hand eleven seasons to somebody else for free.

There was a plant three down that needed it. She saw the lean in it from four feet away, the way one side had reached for a gap in the greenhouse frame and thrown the whole architecture of the thing off center, and her hand came up with the shears the way it always did, and then it stopped, an inch from the stem, hovering, and she stood there long enough that Wyatt noticed and straightened up from his own row to see what she was looking at.

"Something wrong with it?"

"No," she said. "It's leaning is all."

She could have left it at that. There was a version of the sentence that ended there and the morning went on exactly as it had, and nobody in that greenhouse or anywhere else would ever have known there was a version where it didn't.

Instead she found herself standing with the shears still an inch off the stem, turning over what it would cost her to say the next part, and found, for the first time since she'd hired him, that the answer wasn't nothing. It wasn't the eleven seasons — those were already spent, already hers whether or not she ever said a word about them. What it would cost her was smaller and stranger: it was admitting, by the act of teaching it, that she'd started keeping score of who might still be standing here after she wasn't, and that until this exact moment she had not let herself do that arithmetic out loud, not with Wyatt, not with Carrie, not with anyone.

"Come here," she said. "I want to show you something you won't need most years. But when you need it, you'll need it exact."

He came and stood at her shoulder and she talked him through it slow, the way she wished someone had talked her through it back when she'd been finding it the hard way, plant by ruined plant, across most of her thirties — where to cut, how far below the lean, how to read whether the plant would stall or recover in the first forty-eight hours after, what a stalled plant looked like at hour six versus hour twelve versus the point past which it wasn't coming back. He asked two questions in the middle of it and went quiet for the rest, watching her hands the way he watched everything, like a man trying to memorize something before it was taken off the table.

When she was done she handed him the shears and had him do the next one himself, and his cut was close enough to right that she didn't correct it, only told him what she would have done differently and why, and let him carry the difference forward into the one after that.

They finished the east row a little before noon and broke for water, and she did not tell him what she'd just done, and he did not ask her to name it, and the two of them stood at the end of the row in the thinning fog with the plants behind them starting the six weeks of work neither of them would see the end result of for months. She took the shears back from him when he offered them, wiped the blades clean on her jeans out of habit, and then did not put them back in her own bag. She set them on the bench at his end of the row instead, where he'd find them Monday without either of them having to say why they were there.

By the second week of July she had stopped counting what she gave him and started teaching the way she'd once been taught, which was to say without accounting for it, one thing folded into the next until neither of them could have said on any given day where instruction ended and the ordinary work of the season began.
