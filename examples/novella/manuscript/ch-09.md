The checklist lived in a manila folder now instead of loose on the table, eleven pages Ruth had three-hole-punched herself on a Sunday night, and she started at the top the way she started rows in the field, left to right, no skipping ahead to the easy ones.

Item one was the generator shed. Thirty-eight feet from the boundary where the county wanted fifty, and of everything on the list it looked, on paper, like the item a woman could fix with her own two hands and a Saturday. She walked the distance with a hundred-foot tape and a stake at each end, twice, to be sure, and then she stood in the gap between where the shed sat and where it needed to sit and understood that twelve feet was not a number so much as a direction, and the direction ran uphill, into a slope she'd have to cut into before she could set a new pad.

She called the county back to ask what a person needed to move a shed twelve feet. The woman on the phone was not unkind. She said a structure over 120 square feet required a building permit regardless of whether it was new construction or relocation, and that any grading disturbance over fifty cubic yards on a slope in excess of thirty percent required a geotechnical report stamped by a licensed engineer, and Ruth's slope, she was fairly sure without even pulling the parcel map, would qualify. Ruth wrote *geotech report* in the margin next to *shed* and did not ask what that cost, because she had learned by August not to ask a question until she was ready for the answer.

She moved to item four instead, the irrigation lines, which had no engineer standing between her and the paperwork, only patience, and patience she still had in full. She spent three mornings with Wyatt and a handheld GPS unit borrowed from Hollis Meeker, walking every foot of half-inch poly from the intake to the last drip stake in the north block, marking a waypoint every twenty feet the way the county's as-built form required, and by the third morning her knees complained on the uphill stretches in a way they hadn't ten years before, but the map filled in, waypoint by waypoint, and that felt like something she could finish. She printed it Thursday night on the inkjet in the office, forty-one pages of coordinates, and set it in the folder under a tab she labeled *irrigation — complete*, and allowed herself, for the length of one cup of coffee, to feel something like progress.

Item seven was the full-sun canopy, the 17,400 square feet the county's overlay maps had marked unpermittable regardless of anything Ruth did to the structures around it, and this one, at least, cost her nothing but the plants themselves. She and Wyatt spent a hot Tuesday pulling drip stakes and rolling back weed cloth from the excess rows, moving the plants that could still be moved into containers under the existing mixed-light structure, and composting the rest where they stood, sixty-some plants she'd started from clone in April going into the pile behind the equipment shed instead of into a fall harvest. Wyatt didn't ask why. He just worked the row across from her, and when they were done the footprint on the county's map and the footprint on the ground matched for the first time in Ruth's memory, and she stood at the edge of the cleared rows a long moment before she went back to the house, because compliance, it turned out, was not always a technical problem. Sometimes it was only a shovel and the willingness to use it on something you'd grown.

That left the dam.

She drove up to the intake on a Thursday in early August with the checklist folder on the passenger seat and Deb Ostrander's card clipped to page nine, and she stood on the bank where the water came down off the ridge and looked at the structure she and a man named Carl Jespersen had built with two come-alongs and a Saturday's worth of concrete in 1987, and she tried to see it the way the county's engineer would see it: seventy-four feet from a required hundred, no fish passage, no measured bypass flow, a diversion built by two people who had wanted, more than anything, not to be seen.

Deb had told her, that first week they'd talked, in late July, what a Class II remediation would likely run — an engineered intake structure with a fish screen and a documented bypass, plans stamped and submitted, a construction season if the permit came through at all — and Ruth had let the number sit unexamined in the back of her mind the way she let unopened mail sit on the table, present but not yet true. She called three contractors that week who did the kind of stream-crossing and diversion work the county's list required. Two didn't call back. The third, a firm out of McKinleyville that Deb had recommended by name, sent a man named Perkins up to walk the creek with her in the last week of July, and he was polite and thorough and took photographs and measurements the way Corinne Yee had, and eleven days later a bid arrived in Ruth's inbox with a cover letter and a number, and the number was a hundred and seventy-one thousand dollars, engineering and permitting fees not included.

She read it standing at the kitchen counter with her laptop propped against the bread box, and she read it a second time, and then she went and got a pencil and did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding all season, because a number on a screen was not real to her the way a number written by her own hand was real. She wrote her best year's net, the good year, 2019, before the market broke — fifty-eight thousand, and that was a good year. She divided a hundred and seventy-one thousand by fifty-eight thousand and got a number just under three, and she sat with that, three years of her best years, gone into a concrete structure and a fish screen on a creek that ran, most Augusts, thin enough to step across.

She did not cry over the pencil, and she did not call Perkins back to negotiate, because there was nothing in the number to negotiate with; it was not a price she'd been asked to pay, it was a fact she'd been shown, the way the county's setback tape had shown her twelve feet she couldn't argue with either. She closed the laptop and sat a while at the table where the application still lived, signed now and waiting on a stamp of its own, and she understood, the way she understood a field going to seed a week too late to stop it, that the list she'd started at the top of in July was not a list she was going to finish. Not because she'd failed an item on it. Because the list itself, taken whole, had never been sized for a woman working fifty-eight acres with one part-time hand and a pencil, and no one at the county had needed to tell her no. The arithmetic had done it for them.

She put the pencil down and went out to check the propagation house before dark, the way she did every evening whether there was anything to check or not, and the trays were where Wyatt had left them, each one set down carefully after being looked at instead of before, and she stood in the doorway a minute with the folder still open in her mind, eleven pages, four items crossed off clean and one that could not be crossed off at any price she had, and she did not decide anything that night. She only noted, the way she noted frost or a shift in the wind, that a decision was now closer than it had been that morning, and that no one was going to make it for her by telling her no. They were only going to let the numbers keep doing it, quietly, until she said yes to something else instead.
