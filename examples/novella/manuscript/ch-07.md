The trellis line ran eighty feet along the south block, four-foot stakes and a grid of baling twine Ruth had been restringing every June for thirty-one years, and by seven the dew had already burned off enough that the plants would forgive being handled. She showed Wyatt the tuck first, the way you took a branch that wanted to grow up and out past the canopy and laid it sideways instead, weaving it under one line and over the next so the plant did the work of holding itself down.

"You're not bending it," she said. "You're asking it. There's a difference in how much it fights you back."

Wyatt tucked a branch, straightened, tucked the next one over. He was quick with his hands, quicker than most people she'd started, and he didn't flinch from the resin the way some did the first few weeks, the way it blacked their fingertips and wouldn't come off with soap, only time. He'd learned to top clean by the second week of June. He'd learned, without being told twice, that a leaf yellowing from the bottom up was nitrogen and a leaf yellowing from the top down was something else entirely, and he wrote neither of those things down, because by then he didn't need to.

What he had not learned, and what she watched for now the way she watched for spider mite, was the specific patience the tuck required past the fifth or sixth branch, when the shape of the plant stopped being obvious and you had to feel for where the next redirection ought to go rather than see it. He kept doing the easy tucks and skipping past the ones that needed thought, moving down the row faster than the row wanted to be moved.

"Slow down on this one," she said, and put her hand flat against a branch he'd have missed, low and heavy with a bud site that would shade out three others if it grew where it wanted to. "This is the one that decides the whole cola. You rush past this one, you'll be untangling it in September and it'll be too late to fix without cutting something you needed."

He came back and looked at it a long moment, the way she'd taught him to look, and found the line and tucked it, and it was right.

"There," she said. "That's the part nobody sees in October. October just looks like a good plant."

"Huh," he said, not unkindly, already moving on.

She went back to her own side of the row. It was a good morning for it, overcast enough that the plants wouldn't wilt from the handling, and for a while the only sound was the twine creaking against the stakes and the two of them breathing through the work, and she let herself think, the way she allowed herself to think about it now only in pieces, small enough to carry, that this was the shape a life could take if a person let it: eighty feet of trellis, a boy who could learn, a body of knowledge passed hand to hand instead of buried. She had not let herself want that in so many words. She let herself notice it was there, the way you noticed weather.

"Canopy's got a job posting up in Willow Creek," Wyatt said, not looking up from the branch he was working. "Trellis and integrated pest, twenty-two an hour to start. Benefits after ninety days."

Ruth kept her hands moving. "That so."

"Yeah, my buddy Dane applied. They do dental. Retirement match, three percent." He tucked another branch, checked it, moved on. "Twenty-two's more than I make now."

"It is," she said. It was, by a fair margin, and she did not argue the number, because the number was not wrong any more than the six dollars and seventy-seven cents an hour had been wrong. It was only a number describing something that had nothing to do with what she was trying to hand him.

"You gonna apply," she said. It came out level, not quite a question.

Wyatt shrugged, the particular shrug of a twenty-three-year-old who had not yet decided a thing was a decision. "Dunno. Maybe. Steady's steady." He said it the way he might have said the weather was steady, information, not intention, and went back to the branch in front of him, and that was the whole of it, no drama in his voice at all, which was somehow the part that stayed with her longest — that he could hold in one hand a wage with dental and in the other a technique it had taken her eleven years to learn well enough to teach, and set them down side by side on the same morning without weighing one against the other, because to him they weren't the same kind of thing. One was a job. The other was a job too, as far as he was concerned, just one that happened to also teach you things, the way any job might, and if a better one came along paying twenty-two dollars an hour with dental, a person took it, and there was nothing in that reasoning she could call wrong, because it was the reasoning of every sensible twenty-three-year-old she had ever known, herself included, once, before the reasoning had stopped applying to her without her noticing exactly when.

She finished her side of the row before he finished his, which had not happened in three weeks, and she stood at the end of it a moment with her hand on the last stake, looking back down the line at the branches laid low and orderly under the twine, each one asking rather than bent, and understood that she had spent the last month teaching a man to do a thing well who would, in all likelihood, do it well for someone else, or do something else entirely, and that neither of those outcomes was a fault in him. He had never told her he was staying. She had only, somewhere in the cutting and the topping and the long unhurried afternoons of it, begun to build a shape in her mind that had his hands in it doing this ten years on, twenty, the way Denny Calloway's hands were still, in her memory, doing what his father had taught him on a ridge two miles from here, and she had built that shape herself, out of nothing he had promised her, and it was hers to take down again.

She did not say any of this. She said, "Water's at three today, not four, ground's still wet from Tuesday," and Wyatt said okay, and they moved on to the next block, and the tuck he did there was clean, and she told him so, and meant it, and the two things — the cleanness of the tuck and the quiet, settling knowledge that he would carry it somewhere else — sat in her without touching each other for the rest of the day, the way two tools could lie side by side in a drawer without ever once being used for the same job.

By the end of July she had stopped, without deciding to, mentioning the ten-year plantings, the varieties that wanted a decade of selection to get right, the things a person only bothered explaining to someone they expected to still be standing beside them when the decade was up. She still taught him everything asked for and a good deal that wasn't, thoroughly, correctly, no different than she'd ever taught it. In August she filled out the trellis order for next June — four cases of twine, same as always — and left the line for a fifth case blank, and mailed it.
