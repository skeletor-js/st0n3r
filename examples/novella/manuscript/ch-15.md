The barn held the smell of the whole year at once—diesel from the generator, cedar shavings under the drying racks, and beneath both of those, the crop itself, sharp and sweetish, the smell she had learned before she'd learned almost anything else about the work. Ruth worked down the row of hanging stems with her thumb and forefinger, testing the small stalks for snap. Most gave the dry click she wanted, wood-brittle, ready. A few near the south wall still bent instead of breaking, and she left those hanging, made a note on the pad nailed to the post, and moved on.

She had four hundred and eleven plants worth of material to bring down, cure, and jar, and she had been doing it alone since the twenty-ninth, when Wyatt drove to Willow Creek for the first of two mornings of paperwork and orientation videos before his job started in earnest. He had offered to push the date. She had told him not to. There was nothing left in the barn that required two sets of hands, only two sets of days, and she had the days.

The cure itself she had learned from Alta Furse in the fall of 1998, in a barn colder than this one, on a ridge two watersheds over that had since been sold twice and subdivided once. Alta had shown her the burping schedule on a legal pad in handwriting Ruth could still see when she closed her eyes — open the jars twice a day the first week, once a day the second, every other day the third, until the humidity read where it needed to read and stayed there without help. Alta had made her do it by feel before she let her touch a hygrometer, had made her learn what sixty-two percent felt like against the pad of a thumb before any machine confirmed it. Ruth had never taught anyone that part. Not Wyatt, not the two men before Wyatt whose names she still had in a ledger somewhere. It was the one piece of the work she had kept back. It took a year to teach right, and she had never yet had anyone worth a year.

She brought down the first cart of dried stems at eight and had it stripped and jarred by eleven, sixty-four quart jars lined on the workbench with masking tape labels — block, date, row — in the same square hand she'd used on jars since before Iris was born. She burped the first batch at noon, cracking each lid a half turn, holding her wrist over the mouth of the jar the way Alta had taught her, feeling for the give of moisture against skin rather than reading a gauge. Two jars ran hot. She set them aside, lids off, for another hour under the fan, and did not think of them again until the fan told her to.

The permit folder was on the kitchen table, where it had been since Eureka, closed. She had not opened it since Pelletier read the decision standing up. She knew without opening it that the license covered eight thousand square feet, that it took effect in the spring, that it applied to next year's planting and not to a single stem hanging in this barn tonight. The crop curing under her hands had grown on ground the county had, as of three weeks ago, formally declined to recognize as fully hers to use. It did not know that. It cured the same regardless, on its own schedule, indifferent to Pelletier, to Corinne Yee's tablet, to the eight thousand square feet or the twenty-eight thousand it did not cover. She had grown it before the ruling and it would finish becoming what it was going to become without asking the ruling's permission, and there was something in that fact she turned over once, standing at the workbench with a jar in each hand, and then set down, because turning it over further wouldn't change the moisture reading.

She worked through the afternoon in the order Alta had taught her — heaviest colas first, because they held moisture longest and needed the earliest jarring to avoid mold at the stem, then the mid-canopy material, then the small buds off the lower branches that most growers she'd known over the years had thrown into trim bins without a second look. Ruth had never done that. Alta hadn't either. The small stuff cured faster and, handled right, came out with a tighter, denser burn than half the canopy tops people bragged about at the co-op meetings back when there'd been co-op meetings. She jarred it separately, labeled it separately, because someone — she did not let herself finish the thought about who — might want to know one day that the distinction existed and had been kept.

At four the light started going out of the barn the way it did now, fast and gray, November light with nothing behind it. She turned on the two hanging bulbs over the workbench and kept going. Her knees told her, crouching to reach the low racks, that she was sixty-one, information she already had and did not need repeated, and she straightened between racks more than she used to, a hand flat against the small of her back, and then went on.

By six she had reached the end of the second cart and sat on the overturned bucket she kept by the door for exactly this, when there was nothing left to do but wait for the next burp cycle. She ate a sandwich she'd made that morning and not eaten, cold now, standing over the sink water she'd never bothered to run in the barn, and looked at the sixty-four jars on the bench, then the empty racks where the stems had hung that morning, then out the open door at the dark coming down over the south block where, in April, whatever she planted next would have to fit inside a boundary drawn on a county map instead of one she'd walked and judged with her own eye for forty years.

She did not decide, sitting there, what the eight thousand square feet meant for the rest of it — for the acreage outside the line, for the dam, for whether she'd spend the winter chasing engineering estimates she couldn't pay for or let the unlicensed ground go fallow and see what grew back on it that wasn't hers to grow. She had time before spring to decide that, and the deciding, she understood without much feeling about it either way, would take the same shape all her decisions this year had taken: numbers on a page, weighed slowly, acted on late. Tonight there was only the cure, and the cure did not care what she decided.

She finished the last cart on the ninth of November, a Wednesday, working by the two bulbs until nearly nine, jarring the final small-bud batch off the north block by feel with her hands cold enough that she had to breathe on them twice. She wrote the date on the last label, set the jar with the others, and stood a moment looking at the full bench — a hundred and eight jars in all, more than she had made in three years, the whole record of the season reduced to glass and weight and a schedule of burping that would run another three weeks before any of it was finished enough to sell, smoke, or set aside.

Nobody had watched her do it. Corinne Yee's rangefinder had no use here; Pelletier's commission had no jurisdiction over a thumb held to a jar mouth, testing for give. It was, when she thought about it walking back to the house under a sky gone fully dark, the only part of the year that had not been measured by anyone but her, and it would not appear on any application, any report, any spreadsheet with a buyer's columns waiting to be filled. She let herself in, hung her jacket on the hook by the door, and did not go check the compliance folder on the table, though she passed within a foot of it on her way to bed.

The cure ran its full three weeks without incident. She burped the jars on schedule through the rest of November, morning and evening the first week, once a day the second, every other day the third, exactly as Alta had taught her twenty years before either of them had a reason to believe the state would ever ask to see a single jar of it. By the first week of December the humidity held where it needed to hold without her help, sixty-two percent across a hundred and eight jars, checked by thumb before she trusted the gauge, and she sealed the lids down for the last time and carried the full cases out to the storage room off the barn, one at a time, the way she had carried them every December for longer than she could any longer easily count.
