Iris arrived on a Friday in the first week of August, driving her own car up from the Bay Area instead of flying into Arcata and asking Ruth to fetch her, which Ruth noted and did not mention. She came in windows down, dust from the last mile of gravel road already on her forearms, and she hugged Ruth in the driveway the way she had since she was twelve, a quick clamp and release, before she went around to the trunk for her bag.

"You look thin," Ruth said.

"I look forty-three," Iris said. "There's a difference."

Ruth took her through the south block before dinner because that was what she did with visitors, family or not, and because she wanted her daughter to see the plants at their best, heavy now and staked, the smell of them thick enough in the afternoon heat that Iris put a hand up to her face and laughed and said it never stopped being strange to her, how much it smelled like somewhere she used to live. Ruth showed her the trellis work Wyatt had finished the week before, clean lines, no crushed stems, and she found herself narrating the way she narrated for Wyatt, the why under the what — how tucking too early snapped a cola, how you waited for the stem to lignify before you asked it to bend. Iris nodded along the row the way she'd nodded as a teenager, patient, a little removed, a woman being shown a language she had once spoken and had let go slack in her mouth.

"You still know all of it," Iris said. It was not quite a compliment.

"I know more of it than I did last year," Ruth said. "Funny thing, at my age."

They ate on the porch because the kitchen held heat past dark in August, and Ruth had made the thing Iris used to ask for as a girl, a pot of beans with the ham hock, cornbread on the side, and for a while they talked about nothing that mattered — Iris's husband Paul and his knee surgery, the boys' summer camp, a house two streets over from Iris's that had sold for a number that made Ruth put her fork down. It was only after the plates were cleared and the light had gone the color of weak tea over the ridge that Iris brought up the offer, and she brought it up carefully, the way she'd once broached a scraped car door or a bad grade, testing the ground before she put her weight on it.

"Denny sold," she said. "Mom told me. I mean — you told me. But I looked him up. There's a listing already, the parcel's on some investor site. Fourteen a acre, it says."

"That's about right."

"That's not a small offer."

"It's not," Ruth said.

Iris turned her glass a quarter turn on the table, not drinking from it, just turning it, a habit Ruth remembered from childhood report cards and college applications, the stalling gesture before the thing she actually meant to say. "I've been doing the math since you called about it in June," Iris said. "You could pay off whatever's left on the note, you could put the rest somewhere that isn't sixty acres of liability, and you could — I don't know, Mom, rest. You could actually rest. You've never once in my life rested."

"I don't know what I'd do with a year of resting."

"You could figure it out. That's kind of the point."

Ruth looked out at the dark line of the ridge instead of at her daughter, and she thought of the checklist folded now into the back of the folder, four items crossed off and one that could not be, and she thought that she had not told Iris about the dam bid, about the hundred and seventy-one thousand dollars, because she had not wanted the number to do her daughter's arguing for her before Iris had said her own piece. She wanted, though she would not have put it in those words to anyone, some sign that Iris saw what Ruth saw when she walked the rows — not a liability, not sixty acres of risk, but forty years of a particular knowledge that had a shape and a weight and might, if someone wanted it, be handed on.

"You could learn it," Ruth said. "If you wanted. I taught Wyatt the pruning method this summer, the one I never taught anybody. Took him about six weeks to get his hands to do what his eyes already understood. You picked things up faster than that, always did."

Iris was quiet long enough that a truck passed on the county road below, its lights sweeping the fence line and gone. "Mom," she said, and her voice had dropped the careful register it had held all evening, gone flat and plain the way Ruth's own voice went when she meant something completely. "I never wanted the farm. I didn't want it at fifteen, sneaking off the property because I was so sick of the smell of it on my own clothes, and I don't want it now. I love you. I'm not going to pretend I love this."

The porch light buzzed with a moth against the bulb and neither of them moved to swat it away. Ruth had known this, she understood sitting there, had known it in the way she knew weather a day out, by the pressure and the color of the sky, and had simply not let herself say the word for it until her daughter said it for her. She thought of the ten years she had built without meaning to around Wyatt's steady hands, and how quietly that fantasy had folded itself flat back in July, and she understood now that this had been the same fantasy wearing a different face — a hope she had never examined closely enough to know she was carrying it, that somewhere in her own blood the work would go on being wanted the way she had wanted it.

"All right," Ruth said.

"That's it? All right?"

"What did you want me to say."

Iris's shoulders came down an inch, some argument she'd braced to have gone slack for lack of a target. "I don't know. I wanted you to fight me on it, maybe. I've been ready for you to fight me on it since I got in the car."

"I'm sixty-one," Ruth said. "I've been fighting the county all summer. I don't have it in me to fight you too, and I don't think I want to. You never owed me a reason to love a thing I loved."

They sat with that a while, the moth giving up on the bulb, crickets starting up thick in the field grass below the porch rail, and Ruth found she did not feel the grief she might have expected, only a kind of settling, the way a fence post settled once it had been tamped and left to the frost. She had asked the question of her daughter without quite letting herself hear it as a question, and now it was answered, plainly, and there was a mercy in that even if it was not the mercy she'd hoped for. Iris reached over at some point and put her hand flat on the table near Ruth's, not quite touching, and left it there until the crickets were the only sound and it was time to take the dishes in.

Iris stayed through Sunday. She helped Wyatt haul feed sacks on Saturday morning without being asked and complained about her back at lunch, and in the afternoon she sat on an overturned bucket in the shade of the propagation house and watched Ruth work a tray of clones, and once, when Ruth held up a cutting to show the node placement, Iris leaned in and looked at it the way a person looks at a photograph of a place they used to live, with interest and no claim on it at all. She left Sunday evening the way she'd come, dust already collecting on the hood before she'd cleared the gate, and Ruth stood in the driveway and watched the taillights go small and then gone around the bend by the Meekers' fence line.

She went back into the house and sat at the kitchen table where the folder still lived, and she understood, without needing to write it down or say it aloud to anyone, that whatever answer this land was going to get, it was not going to come from a daughter or a grandchild carrying it forward the way it had come to her, unbroken, from nobody — her mother had grown tomatoes and beans and nothing stronger, and Ruth had built the whole of it herself, alone, out of stubbornness and forty summers of paying close attention. If there was a hand it passed to now, it was a hired hand's, temporary, headed for Willow Creek and twenty-two dollars an hour with dental, and beyond that there was no one. She was, she thought, turning the porch light off and going down the dark hall to bed, the only person left alive who knew the whole of it, start to finish, root to cure, and whatever she decided this fall, she would decide it as exactly that — the last one, and no one behind her to ask why.
