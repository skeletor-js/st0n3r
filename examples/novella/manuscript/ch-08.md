The road to Denny Calloway's place had been graded twice since spring, which was the first thing Ruth noticed, because for eleven years before that it hadn't been graded at all, and a man who let his road wash out every winter for a decade did not suddenly pay to have it smoothed unless someone besides him was going to be driving on it.

She parked behind a flatbed she didn't recognize, backed up to what used to be his propagation greenhouse, and two men she didn't recognize either were loading racked trays of clones onto it, wearing lanyards with a logo she recognized fine. She sat in the truck a moment with the engine off, looking at the lanyards, and then she got out anyway, because she had come forty minutes for this and she wasn't going to turn around over a flatbed.

Denny came out of the barn wiping his hands on a rag that had given up being white sometime around the turn of the century, same as he had, more or less, sixty-eight now to her sixty-one, his shoulders still doing the work but slower about it. He'd taught her how to clone in 1985, standing over her hands at his own bench, telling her the cut had to be at an angle or the plant would drown itself trying to heal a flat wound, and she had never forgotten it and had taught it to Wyatt in the same words three weeks ago without meaning to quote him.

"Ruth." He didn't look surprised. "Didn't figure you for a social call."

"I'm not much for those," she said. "Whose trucks."

"Canopy's. Closed Tuesday." He said it the way he might have said he'd changed his oil.

She looked past him at the racks going onto the flatbed, four hundred clones at least, rooted and healthy, his genetics, some of them lines he'd been running since before she'd met him. "They taking the mothers too."

"They're taking everything that's alive and everything that's built. That was the deal." He folded the rag and set it on a fence post, an old habit, tools and rags always put down somewhere they could be found again, and she understood without him saying it that the habit would outlast the barn he was folding it next to. "Come on in, I got coffee that's only this morning's."

The kitchen still had his wife's crocheted potholders on the hooks by the stove, though Marilyn had been gone six years now, and Ruth sat at a table that had a coffee ring worn permanently into the wood at the place where a mug had always gone, and Denny poured two cups without asking how she took hers, because he'd known for thirty years.

"I came because I thought you'd fight it," she said. There was no good way into it, so she went in flat. "I thought if anybody on this ridge was going to tell them no, it'd be you."

Denny sat down heavy, the chair taking his weight with a sound like it had done this exact thing ten thousand times before. "Fight what, exactly."

"The permit. The setbacks. The whole — " She stopped, because she heard herself starting to list, the way she'd started listing to Corinne Yee's clipboard, and she didn't want to do that here, not to him. "You had forty acres running clean for thirty years. You knew that ground better than the county surveyor knows his own truck."

"I did." He drank his coffee, looked into the cup like there might be something else in it. "Ruth, I got the letter same as you got yours. Eleven pages, same as yours I'd guess. I read the part about the setback on my dam and I knew right off — right off, inside ten seconds — I wasn't fixing that, not at my age, not for what it'd cost me. Sat with it. Four days, maybe five. Then Corinne Yee called, same as she called you I expect, and I — " He shrugged. "I took the number."

"Nine thousand an acre? Fourteen?"

"Fourteen. Active application." Said it flat, no embarrassment in it, which was somehow worse than if there had been. "Same math you're looking at, I'd guess. Elsewise you'd be home."

She turned the mug a quarter turn on the table, not drinking from it. "You could've held out. Made them come get you the hard way. Forced a hearing, forced a variance fight, made it cost them something."

"To what end." Not really a question, and he wasn't unkind saying it. "I'm sixty-eight. My knees tell me that every morning whether I ask 'em or not. I don't have a Wyatt, Ruth. Got a nephew in Fresno calls twice a year, wants nothing to do with any of it — not the land, not the work, none of it. So who was I holding the line for. Tell me that."

She didn't have an answer for that one that didn't sound like an accusation, so she let it sit.

"You want to know what I think." Denny said it like he was deciding whether to bother. "Since you drove all the way out here, I'll tell you, and you can be sore about it or not, that's yours to be." He set the cup down, turned it once. "Thirty-some years I ran from the DEA. County sheriff. Forest Service chopper come over twice, low, right over that barn. Hid the water lines under brush. Timed my burn pile so it wouldn't show on a flyover. Got good at hiding, Ruth. Real good. Had to be — man with a badge finds you, you lose it all, just like that." He stopped, rubbed his jaw. "This time nobody came up the road with a warrant. Just a letter. Eleven pages, like I told you, and I read every one of 'em standing right there in the mudroom and knew inside ten seconds I'd already lost it, only it was going to take longer to admit and cost more on the way. Way I see it, they didn't end prohibition, Ruth. They just put a tie on it and sent it up the road with a lawyer instead of a badge." He got up, went to the fence post, and picked the rag back up, folded it again though it didn't need folding. "Same amount gone, I expect, whichever way a man goes about it — just took its own time getting there instead of all at once."

The furnace kicked on somewhere behind the wall, an old sound in an old house, and neither of them said anything for a moment while it ran.

"I keep waiting for the part where somebody tells me I'm wrong about that," Ruth said finally.

"Nobody's going to tell you that." He refilled his own cup, didn't offer to top off hers, because she hadn't touched it. "You ain't wrong, I don't guess, but being right about it don't seem to buy a person much." He leaned against the counter. "You didn't drive out here for my coffee. What'd you actually want from me, Ruth."

She thought about it honestly before she answered, which she gave him credit for deserving. "I wanted somebody who'd been in it as long as I have to tell me there was a way through that didn't end in a check."

"Wasn't one for me." He shrugged, an old man's shrug, economical, no wasted motion in it even now. "Might be one for you. You got the boy. Got ten more years in your legs than I got in mine." A pause. "Or maybe there ain't one, and you take the check anyway, and I don't figure that makes you a quitter, Ruth — just numbers, same as anything else." He drank again. "I signed my name Tuesday. Slept fine that night. First time in — Christ, fifteen years maybe — I didn't wake up at three in the morning listening for a truck on the road that wasn't mine."

She left without finishing the coffee, and he didn't seem to mind, walked her out past the flatbed where the men in Canopy lanyards were strapping down the last of his mother plants, forty years of selection going out on a chain binder, and he raised a hand to her the same easy way he always had, like it was any other Tuesday she was driving off his land, and she raised hers back.

On the drive home she did not think about the flatbed, or the potholders, or even the coffee ring worn into the table. She thought about Denny folding a rag that didn't need folding, the way his hands had gone to that small, ordinary task the same second his voice had gone flat and final, and she found she could not set the image down the way she'd set down other things people had told her that summer, the fee schedules, the setback numbers, the offer with its expiration date, because those had all been outside her, things done to the land, and this one had lodged somewhere she couldn't fence off from the rest of it. She thought too of the plain, flat way he'd said it — that they hadn't ended prohibition so much as dressed it in a collar and a tie and sent it up the road with a lawyer instead of a badge — and she could not, driving the graded road down to the county highway, find the flaw in it, though she went on looking for one the rest of the way home. She had thought, without ever quite saying it to herself, that her anger these past weeks was a private thing, hers alone, maybe even a little foolish, an old woman unable to let go of a way of doing business that the world had simply outgrown. Denny had lived the same forty years beside her, on the next ridge over, hiding the same water lines, and he had folded the rag and signed the paper in the same afternoon, and she could not decide, driving the graded road back down to the county highway, whether that made him the wiser one or simply the more tired.

The truck was still ticking when she cut the engine in her own yard, and Wyatt was up at the propagation house with the doors open, moving trays under the lights the way she'd shown him, checking each one before he set it down instead of after, which was the difference between doing a job and having learned it.

He looked up when she came across the gravel. "How was Denny's."

"Sold." She said it plain, the way Denny would have said it. "Canopy's got it. All of it — the mothers, the clones, the whole forty acres, near as I could tell."

Wyatt didn't say anything for a second, just held the tray he was holding. "Guess that's — " He stopped, started again. "They ask you? Canopy. About me?"

She looked at him a beat before she understood what he meant, and then she understood it, and something in her chest that had been tight all the way down the ridge road came a little looser, though not in a good direction. "They offer you something?"

"Recruiter called Tuesday. Same day, I guess, if Denny — " He shrugged, set the tray down careful, the way she'd taught him. "Didn't really say anything. Wanted to hear what you'd say first, I guess, before I said anything back."

"That's not how you should be making that decision," she said, and meant it, and was aware even as she said it that it cost her something to mean it.

"I know." He didn't look away from her. "Asking anyway."

She could have told him about the coffee ring in Denny's kitchen, or the flatbed, or the mother plants going out on a chain binder, and let him draw his own conclusions the slow way, the way she'd had to. Instead she told him the true thing, which was smaller and harder to say. "I'm not selling. I don't know yet if that's brave or just stubborn, and I'm not going to pretend I do. But I'm not selling, and if you stay, you're staying for a woman with a permit application on her kitchen table and no guarantee the county doesn't tell her no anyway."

Wyatt nodded slow, like he was turning it the way she'd taught him to turn a stem, checking the cut. "Okay," he said, which was not agreement exactly and not the Canopy job either, and she let it be enough for one evening.

Inside, the application was where she'd left it three weeks before, the eleven pages Corinne Yee's clipboard had made feel like a verdict, and she sat down at her own table — no ring worn into this one yet, though she supposed one would come — and she read through the setback measurements one more time, and then she found her pen, and she signed the last page where the county wanted her signature, and she did not wake at three that morning listening for a truck, because for the first time in a long while there wasn't one she was waiting to hear.

In the morning she carried her coffee up to the propagation house before the dew had burned off the gravel, and found Wyatt already at the bench, checking trays the way she'd shown him. She told him she'd signed. He nodded, set down the tray he was holding, and didn't ask her to say more than that. "You call the recruiter back yet?" she asked, though she'd told herself on the walk up that she wouldn't. "Nope," he said. "Not planning to. Not this week, anyway." It wasn't a promise, and she didn't ask him to make it one. She picked up the trellis order she'd started three weeks before, still short a column of measurements she hadn't gotten around to finishing, and set it on the bench between them, and let him watch her start filling it in for spring, the way a person plans for ground they mean to still be standing on.
